\section{Background}

Urbanisation is reshaping mobility at an unprecedented pace: the United Nations projects that 68\% of the world's population will reside in cities by 2050~\parencite{un2018wup}, intensifying congestion, greenhouse gas emissions, and demand for scalable, on-demand transport.  In New York City alone, taxis and ride-hailing vehicles account for hundreds of millions of trips annually, contributing substantially to urban congestion and vehicular emissions~\parencite{taxinyc_dataset}.  Shared-ride taxi services sit at the intersection of these pressures, capable of door-to-door coverage that mass transit cannot provide, yet environmentally costly when vehicles carry a single passenger.  Realising the potential of shared taxis therefore requires two complementary capabilities: accurate \emph{demand forecasting}, enabling operators to pre-position vehicles and reduce idle mileage, and effective \emph{ride pooling}, so that compatible trips share a vehicle and reduce per-passenger cost and emissions.

Taxi demand forecasting is a spatio-temporal prediction problem of significant practical complexity.  Demand varies sharply by time of day, day of week, weather conditions, and geographic zone, requiring models that can capture both linear trends and non-linear interaction effects simultaneously.  The prediction target in this study is the pickup count $y_{c,t}$ within each of $K{=}40$ data-driven spatial clusters and 10-minute temporal bins, yielding thousands of interdependent demand cells per day.  Each cell represents the number of taxi pickups observed in a specific neighbourhood during a specific 10-minute window; predicting this quantity accurately allows operators to estimate how many vehicles are needed in each zone at each moment, rather than reacting to demand only after passengers have already waited.

Operators who can anticipate these counts can dispatch vehicles proactively, reducing passenger wait times and empty repositioning mileage.  In dense urban cores such as Manhattan, a prediction error of even five pickups per bin during the morning rush can translate into visible service degradation: longer queues at kerbside, increased cancellation rates, and higher fuel consumption from vehicles cruising without passengers.  Conversely, over-dispatching to low-demand outer-borough zones wastes driver time and increases fleet operating costs.  The forecasting subsystem of this thesis is therefore designed not merely to minimise a statistical loss function, but to produce actionable dispatch intelligence at neighbourhood granularity.

Ensemble methods have shown consistent superiority in tabular prediction tasks~\parencite{breiman2001rf,chen2016xgboost,wolpert1992stacking}, yet no prior study has systematically compared a comprehensive suite of ensemble strategies on the same spatio-temporal demand task with non-parametric statistical validation.  Individual studies typically champion a single architecture---an LSTM network~\parencite{xu2017realtime}, a gradient-boosted tree~\parencite{liu2021epidemic}, or a hybrid residual model~\parencite{kim2020hybrid}---without establishing whether the reported advantage over alternatives is statistically robust or an artefact of a particular train-test partition.  Ride pooling, independently, requires matching algorithms that balance pool rate, per-trip savings, and realistic passenger-acceptance constraints, yet most operational studies either report theoretical upper bounds~\parencite{santi2014quantifying} or fleet-level outcomes without pairwise economic quantification~\parencite{alonso2017demand}.  The present thesis treats forecasting accuracy and pooling feasibility as jointly necessary conditions for a viable shared-taxi service, and evaluates both under the same data regime, temporal split, and reproducibility standards.

\section{Problem Statement}

Despite substantial progress in urban mobility analytics, shared taxi operators still lack an integrated, empirically validated framework that jointly addresses demand prediction accuracy and operational ride consolidation.  Forecasting models are frequently reported as single-run accuracy figures without establishing whether differences among competing architectures are statistically meaningful~\parencite{kim2020hybrid,liu2021epidemic,xu2017realtime}.  A practitioner comparing an XGBoost model with R\textsuperscript{2}~=~0.984 against an LSTM with MAPE~$\approx$12.5\% has no principled basis for choosing between them, because the studies use different spatial granularities, feature sets, evaluation periods, and reporting metrics.  Pooling studies, conversely, often quantify theoretical shareability under idealised assumptions rather than evaluating greedy matching algorithms that respect detour limits, temporal windows, and directional compatibility~\parencite{santi2014quantifying,agatz2012ridesharing}.  The $\sim$40\% trip-length reduction bound of~\parencite{santi2014quantifying} is frequently cited as evidence that ride sharing is viable, yet no accompanying operational algorithm demonstrates what fraction of that bound survives once passengers refuse detours exceeding 27\% or waits exceeding eight minutes.

The practical consequence is that decision-makers---fleet operators, municipal transport planners, and mobility platform engineers---cannot reliably determine which forecasting strategy to deploy, nor what economic and environmental returns to expect from a constraint-aware pooling service in a city the scale of New York.  Without bootstrap confidence intervals on pool rate and savings, city-scale projections rest on point estimates that may not generalise across sampling variation.  Without non-parametric significance testing across forecasting models, reported accuracy gains may reflect a favourable test month rather than genuine architectural superiority.  This thesis treats these gaps as a single co-design problem: accurate zone-level demand forecasts and realistic pairwise ride matching are complementary levers on the same trip stream, and both must be evaluated under identical data, identical temporal splits, and reproducible statistical protocols.

\section{Research Questions}

Three research questions guide the empirical programme of this thesis.

\textbf{RQ1 (Ensemble benchmarking).}  When fourteen forecasting models---eight individual estimators and six ensemble strategies---are trained and evaluated on NYC Yellow Taxi pickup counts under strictly chronological splits, which architectures achieve the strongest accuracy across a multi-metric suite, and are the observed rank differences statistically significant under non-parametric cross-validation testing?

\textbf{RQ2 (Hybrid residual modelling).}  Does a two-stage LR-LSTM hybrid that separates linear trend extraction from LSTM-based residual modelling improve spatio-temporal demand prediction relative to standalone linear, tree, and deep baselines, and how does an XGBoost-LSTM variant compare when a stronger non-linear first stage replaces linear regression?

\textbf{RQ3 (Constraint-aware ride pooling).}  What pool rate, per-trip savings, and city-scale economic and environmental impact can a multi-criteria greedy matching algorithm achieve on NYC Yellow Taxi trips when five cascading compatibility filters reflecting realistic passenger-acceptance constraints are enforced, and how do these operational outcomes compare with theoretical shareability bounds and naive pairing baselines?

These questions are deliberately scoped to permit falsifiable answers: RQ1 and RQ2 are addressed entirely within the forecasting pipeline of \autoref{ch:methods} and \autoref{ch:framework}, while RQ3 is addressed through Algorithm~\ref{alg:ride_pool} and the bootstrap-validated impact projections reported in \autoref{ch:results}.  Each question specifies not only \emph{what} is measured---accuracy metrics, pool rates, savings---but \emph{how} confidence in the answer is established: through five-fold chronological cross-validation with Friedman testing for RQ1, through controlled comparison of hybrid variants for RQ2, and through 10,000-iteration bootstrap resampling for RQ3.  This explicit linkage between research questions and validation methodology distinguishes the present study from prior work that reports performance without accompanying uncertainty quantification.

\section{Scope and Assumptions}

The empirical scope of this thesis is bounded by the publicly available NYC Yellow Taxi Trip Records~\parencite{taxinyc_dataset}.  Training data comprise January 2015 ($\approx$12.7 million trips); testing data comprise January--March 2016 ($\approx$34 million trips), separated by an 11-month chronological gap to evaluate out-of-distribution generalisation.  Spatial demand is aggregated via MiniBatch KMeans into $K{=}40$ clusters; temporal demand is binned at 10-minute resolution.  Weather features are merged at daily resolution from NOAA, and public holiday indicators are derived from the \texttt{holidays} Python library.  Ride pooling is evaluated on a stratified sample of 106,055 trips rather than the full test corpus, with annual impact figures scaled linearly to the verified 2015 NYC annual volume of $\approx$146 million trips~\parencite{nyc_opendata_2015}.  Key behavioural assumptions include: passengers are willing to share once all five compatibility filters are satisfied; detour estimates use average in-city travel speeds rather than live congestion; and matching is restricted to pairs rather than larger groups.  Graph-based forecasters requiring road-topology data~\parencite{li2018diffusion,liu2023staeformer} are discussed as future directions but lie outside the present experimental scope because the trip records do not include directed road-network adjacency.  Deep trip-origin predictors such as DeepTrip~\parencite{zhang2023deeptrip} and multi-view spatio-temporal models such as DeepMVST~\parencite{yao2018deeptraffic} are similarly excluded from direct comparison because they target different prediction tasks (next-origin classification and multi-view flow grids, respectively) rather than cluster-bin pickup counts.  The scope is intentionally focused on the prediction target and evaluation protocol that enable direct comparison with the Kim et al.~\parencite{kim2020hybrid} LR-LSTM benchmark and the Santi et al.~\parencite{santi2014quantifying} shareability bound, both of which use NYC taxi data and report metrics translatable to the present framework.

\section{Research Gap}

Existing literature has explored each capability in isolation.  Demand-forecasting studies, from early data-driven baselines~\parencite{dia2002object} and streaming prediction approaches~\parencite{moreira2013predicting} to deep spatio-temporal networks~\parencite{zhang2017deep,xu2017realtime,kim2020hybrid}, demonstrate steady accuracy gains, yet rarely validate whether observed improvements across model classes are statistically significant.  Ride-pooling analyses such as the shareability network of~\parencite{santi2014quantifying} and the real-time framework of~\parencite{alonso2017demand} report theoretical upper bounds or fleet-level assignment gains rather than evaluating operational algorithms under realistic passenger-acceptance constraints with pairwise economic quantification.  Ensemble learning contributions~\parencite{wolpert1992stacking,breiman2001rf,chen2016xgboost} have matured independently of both domains, and hybrid deep-boosting architectures~\parencite{zheng2020hybrid} have not been benchmarked alongside six alternative combination strategies on an identical spatio-temporal task.  Neither body of work simultaneously quantifies economic and environmental impact at both the trip and city scale within a single reproducible pipeline.  This thesis addresses all three gaps within a unified framework evaluated on two years of publicly available New York City Yellow Taxi records.

\section{Objectives and Contributions}

This thesis addresses the research questions through three principal contributions.

First, a systematic ensemble benchmarking study evaluates fourteen forecasting models on NYC Yellow Taxi data under strictly chronological splits, directly answering RQ1.  A Friedman non-parametric test confirms that observed differences across model classes are statistically significant, with Stacking achieving R\textsuperscript{2}~=~0.9922 and MAPE~=~10.19\%, establishing a strong upper bound for spatio-temporal taxi demand prediction.  The study is, to the author's knowledge, the first to compare six ensemble strategies---Simple Averaging, Weighted Averaging, Voting Regressor, Stacking, XGBoost-LSTM, and Blending---on identical features, splits, and metrics with accompanying non-parametric validation.

Second, a novel LR-LSTM hybrid architecture is proposed in which a Linear Regression stage extracts the deterministic demand trend and an LSTM stage models the non-linear residuals, addressing RQ2.  An XGBoost-LSTM variant is introduced alongside for comparison, allowing the contribution of each residual-modelling strategy and first-stage choice to be isolated and quantified against the Kim et al.~\parencite{kim2020hybrid} benchmark.

Third, a constraint-aware ride pooling algorithm is developed that applies five cascading compatibility filters reflecting realistic passenger-acceptance requirements identified by~\parencite{furuhata2013ridesharing}, addressing RQ3.  The five filters enforce temporal proximity (within 8 minutes), origin proximity (within 0.75 miles), destination proximity (within 0.75 miles), directional alignment, and a maximum detour ratio of 27\%.  Only trip pairs clearing all five filters receive a weighted compatibility score; a greedy matching pass then selects the highest-scoring pairs within each 5-minute processing window.  The algorithm achieves a 16.06\% pool rate on 106,055 sampled trips, with projected annual savings of USD~28.7~million and approximately 3{,}370~tonnes of CO\textsubscript{2} for New York City scaled from the sample using the verified 2015 NYC Yellow Taxi annual volume of $\approx$146M trips~\parencite{nyc_opendata_2015}, factor $\approx 1{,}377$, validated through bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations.  A supplementary poolability classification experiment (\autoref{sec:pool_eval}) yields a negative result---trip-level features alone cannot predict poolability at booking time---which has direct operational implications for system design.

\section{Thesis Structure and Mapping to Research Questions}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows, with each chapter mapped to the research questions it supports.

\autoref{ch:related} reviews related work in demand forecasting, ride pooling, and ensemble learning, situating the present study within the literature gaps identified above.  \autoref{ch:methods} describes the dataset, preprocessing, spatial clustering, feature engineering, and the ride pooling algorithm; it provides the shared experimental foundation for RQ1--RQ3.  \autoref{ch:framework} presents the proposed EnsembleNet framework, including individual model architectures, the LR-LSTM hybrid (RQ2), and the six ensemble strategies (RQ1).  \autoref{ch:metrics} defines the five-metric evaluation suite and the Friedman--Wilcoxon statistical testing protocol used to validate RQ1.  \autoref{ch:results} reports experimental results for all three research questions and discusses implications, limitations, and sensitivity analyses.  \autoref{ch:conclusion} synthesises findings mapped to RQ1--RQ3, acknowledges limitations, and outlines future directions.

\autoref{fig:intro} presents a high-level overview of the two complementary subsystems that constitute the proposed framework.  The left panel captures the spatial dimension: raw pickup coordinates are partitioned into $K{=}40$ data-driven clusters using MiniBatch KMeans, converting a continuous geographic space into a tractable set of neighbourhood-level demand zones.  Within each zone, demand is aggregated into 10-minute temporal bins, producing a feature matrix that is then enriched with lag features, time-of-day cyclical encodings, weather measurements, and public holiday indicators.  The right panel captures the operational dimension: for any 5-minute window of incoming trips, pairs of rides are evaluated against five cascading filters covering time proximity, origin proximity, destination proximity, route direction alignment, and maximum detour ratio.  Only pairs clearing all five filters receive a weighted compatibility score; a greedy matching pass then assembles the highest-scoring pairs into shared rides.  The two subsystems are designed to operate sequentially in production: demand forecasts inform vehicle pre-positioning, while the pooling algorithm processes the resulting trip requests in real time.  Together they address both the supply-side optimisation problem and the demand-side coordination problem that characterise shared urban mobility.
